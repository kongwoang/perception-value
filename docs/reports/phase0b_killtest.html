Failure Is Not Recoverability

Phase 0 left a testable successor hypothesis: that a frame's value decomposes as criticality × P(cheap fails) × P(full recovers | fails), and that modelling those factors separately beats a monolithic regressor. Half of that hypothesis is true. The half that carries the method contribution is not.

The answerDoes explicit recoverability add value beyond the product baseline?

No — the third factor is inert

Adding p_recover to p_fail × criticality changes nothing: median Δη of −0.002 across 21 held-out sequences, better in only 8 of them, p = 0.73. Against the two-factor gain × criticality the difference is exactly 0.000 (10/21, p = 0.75). The three-factor decomposition is not justified by this data.

The reason is measurable rather than speculative. Failure and recoverability genuinely are different quantities — but the second one is barely predictable from what a cheap model emits, and it is positively correlated with the first, so as a separate multiplicative factor it contributes estimation noise rather than structure.

What did workObject-level learned value, not decomposition

Both uncertainty × criticality (object) and fail × criticality are sums over the same CHEAP candidates, multiplied by the same estimated criticality. The only difference between them is that one uses raw (1 − confidence) as the failure proxy and the other learns it from 35 candidate-level features. That single substitution is worth +0.086 η on 17 of 21 sequences (p = 0.0007).

So there is a real effect here, and it survives the negative test the plan specified — the simple product baseline does not match the learned model. But the effect is located in the failure factor, not in the decomposition, and a two-factor formulation is both simpler and equally strong.

The problem for a method paper

Neither the two-factor nor the three-factor model significantly beats criticality alone (p = 0.14 for both), and criticality alone is a nearly free heuristic. Criterion 5 of the Phase 0B plan is therefore not met. On the plan's own terms this is a WEAK GO: the phenomenon is real and the formulation is defensible, but the method contribution is not yet demonstrated.

Structural findingA ceiling the plan did not anticipate

A deployed gate can only anchor a per-object score on something CHEAP actually emits. Measured over 69,014 rows:

Two consequences. First, a third of the recoverable risk is structurally out of reach of the factorised formulation, which plausibly explains why it cannot pull clear of criticality-only — criticality scores the whole frame and is not subject to that ceiling. Second, most anchorable recoveries appear only as candidates in the [0.10, 0.25) confidence band, i.e. below the operating threshold; a gate that looked only at accepted detections would miss them.

What I recommend

  1. Drop the three-factor model. It is not distinguishable from the two-factor P(compute helps object i) × criticality(i), which is simpler and equally strong.
  2. Attack the 33% anchor ceiling before adding datasets. The most likely reason the factorised model cannot beat criticality-only is that it cannot see a third of the risk. A hybrid — object-anchored term plus a frame-level residual — is the obvious test and costs one experiment.
  3. Only then proceed to a moderate fidelity pair (512 → 640), a second detector family, and a second dataset, in that order.
  4. If the ceiling cannot be broken, position the paper as a problem-formulation and benchmark contribution rather than a method paper. The falsified stakes hypothesis, the risk/detection metric reversal, and the failure-versus-recoverability distinction are each publishable observations.

No RL, no neural scheduler, and no detector optimisation were attempted, per the plan.

What this does not say

  • Not that uncertainty is useless — it is real signal (η@20 = 0.435 versus 0.234 random), it just predicts perception difficulty rather than downstream value of compute.
  • Not that recoverability is a meaningless concept. It is measurably distinct (P(recover | fail) = 0.49 over all objects, range-dependent from 0.33 to 0.79). It is simply not predictable enough, from cheap-side features, to earn its own factor.
  • Not a generalisation claim. This is one dataset, one detector, one fidelity pair. The generalisation tests in the Phase 0B plan have not been run.
